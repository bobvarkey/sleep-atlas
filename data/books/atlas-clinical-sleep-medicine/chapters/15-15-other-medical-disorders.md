## THYROID DISEASE

### Meir H. Kryger

## 15.1

The main function of the thyroid gland is to produce the thyroid hormones triiodothyronine and thyroxine, which play a critical role in the regulation of metabolism. Sleep can be affected by three types of thyroid abnormalities: 1) hyperthyroidism, 2) hypothyroidism, and 3) thyroid mass lesions.

#### HYPERTHYROIDISM

Hyperthyroidism is caused by diseases of the thyroid gland that result in the production of too much thyroid hormone. These include Graves disease, thyroiditis, thyroid nodules, and ingestion of too much iodine or thyroid medication. Less often, in perhaps 1% of cases, hyperthyroidism is caused by a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)–secreting adenoma of the anterior pituitary. In hyperthyroidism, the excessive thyroid hormone results in general symptoms and sleeprelated symptoms and findings, which are described in Table 15.1-1, Boxes 15.1-1 and 15.1-2, and Figures 15.1-1 to 15.1-4.

#### HYPOTHYROIDISM

The clinical features of hypothyroidism are those related to a hypometabolic state and those related to the effect on the cardiovascular and respiratory systems; these are described in Boxes 15.1-3 through 15.1-5, and Figures 15.1-5 to 15.1-8. Hypothyroidism is a risk factor for the development of sleep apnea and is much more common in women than in men. In particular, patients who have been diagnosed with Hashimoto thyroiditis are at risk.

#### ANATOMY

The pituitary gland is located near several structures that are involved in the regulation of sleep (Fig. 15.2-1). Above the pituitary is the hypothalamus, which contains centers involved in the regulation of sleep and temperature, and the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which is the master controller of the circadian rhythm.

Thus any space-occupying disease that involves the pituitary or nearby structures, such as ademomas and crainiopharyngiomas, can affect sleep and may cause sleepiness, insomnia, and temperature dysregulation. In hormone-secreting tumors,

#### Treatment

Besides the treatment of the pituitary adenoma, treatment of the chemical abnormalities has been reported. With bromocriptine, the percentage of sleep time in apnea or hypopnea dropped by 75%. Octreotide, a somatostatin analog, reduced the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) from 39 to 19. Normalization of growth levels after transsphenoidal adenomectomy may cure sleep apnea and improve insulin resistance and hypertension. However, the acromegalic skeletal abnormalities may be irreversible. Also, apneic episodes may continue after normalization of hormonal levels; thus patients may require continuous positive airway pressure therapy.

#### NOCTURNAL GASTROINTESTINAL

#### SYMPTOMS

The manifestation of gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms during sleep is quite familiar to the practicing gastroenterologist. Perhaps the most obvious and common example is the occurrence of epigastric pain that characteristically awakens patients from sleep in the early morning hours or awakens them from sleep with complaints of heartburn or regurgitation. Patients may also have awakenings from sleep with symptoms that ostensibly are not related to GI disorders. For example, sleep disturbance is a common symptom among patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and dyspepsia. Asthmatics may be awakened from sleep by the exacerbation of bronchial asthma, which can be secondary to sleep-related gastroesophageal reflux (GER). Numerous studies are accumulating to suggest that respiratory complications secondary to gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) are common, and that these symptoms are primarily noted as secondary to sleeprelated GER (Box 15.3-1).

#### Upper Gastrointestinal Tract

Despite the recent identification of the role of the bacterium Helicobacter pylori in the pathogenesis of duodenal ulcer disease, acid production remains an important element in ulcer formation and healing. Patients with duodenal ulcer disease maintain a circadian pattern of gastric acid secretion, and studies have shown that the levels of secretion are enhanced in the early part of the sleeping interval. Data on the nocturnal dosing of H2 blockers has documented that nocturnal dosing showed a clear advantage over multiple daily doses. These data strongly support the notion that nocturnal acid suppression alone is sufficient to heal a duodenal ulcer.

#### SLEEP-RELATED GASTROESOPHAGEAL

#### REFLUX

Attention has been focused on the importance of different patterns of GER associated with waking and sleeping. These patterns were documented in studies that involved 24-hour monitoring of the distal esophageal pH. GER is identified when the pH falls below 4 for a period of more than 30 seconds, and the reflux episode is arbitrarily terminated when the pH reaches 4 or 5. Two different patterns of reflux occur: reflux in the upright position occurs most often postprandially and usually consists of two or three reflux episodes that are rapidly cleared (Fig. 15.3-3); reflux in the recumbent position is usually associated with sleep and with more prolonged clearance times (Fig. 15.3-4).

Significant increases in acid mucosal contact times are seen in patients with esophagitis, especially in the recumbent position during sleep as opposed to the upright position. Sleep-related acid contact time appears to play a major role in the development of reflux esophagitis, and reflux during sleep can serve as a stimulus associated with sleep disturbances, which may have a direct effect on esophageal pain sensation Thus it should be recognized that sleep-related GER may be the source of some otherwise unidentified sleep complaints.

#### Pulmonary Complications

Epidemiologic studies have linked symptoms of nighttime heartburn with symptoms of chronic cough, asthma, and sleep-related breathing disturbances. In addition, it has been shown that the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), as assessed by scores on the Berlin Questionnaire, are related to the increased frequency of complaints of nighttime heartburn. Symptoms of nocturnal GER are also associated with daytime sleepiness, daytime tiredness, and fatigue. GER may be clinically “silent”—that is, not associated with a clinical symptom of heartburn—and the treatment of GER may reduce nocturnal asthma symptoms in patients who are refractory to conventional therapy.

#### Intestinal Motility and Lower Bowel Disorders

Attempts to measure bowel motility patterns in patients with IBS have not revealed any discernible pattern that distinguishes this group. However, it has been documented that IBS patients do have sleep complaints, and daytime symptoms may be related to poor sleep. In addition, patients with functional dyspepsia characterized by epigastric postprandial bloating, nausea, or early satiety often complain of general sleep disturbances suggestive of nonrestorative sleep (i.e., numerous awakenings after sleep onset, morning awakenings without feeling rested). It has also been shown that there is a marked increase in sleep complaints in patients with functional bowel disorders, such as IBS and functional dyspepsia, compared with normal controls. In addition, the dyspeptic patients had more complaints of sleep disturbance, perhaps because of more intense abdominal pain. Some studies suggest abnormalities in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and some have shown autonomic imbalances in IBS patients during REM sleep (Fig. 15.3-8), in which increased sympathetic dominance was evident. Thus, not only are there sleep disturbances in this patient population, but the sleep disturbances may also be contributing to altered GI functioning and pain perception. #### CONCLUSIONS

Sleep plays an important role in the manifestation of GI diseases such as GERD, IBS, dyspepsia, and inflammatory bowel disease. Numerous studies support a close link between sleep-related GER and the development of esophagitis. In addition, epidemiologic studies confirm that patients with

nighttime heartburn have an increased risk of various respiratory conditions, such as asthma and OSA. Functional bowel disorders, including nonulcer dyspepsia and IBS, appear to be associated with an increased incidence of sleep complaints, and autonomic dysfunction specifically noted during REM sleep appears to characterize a subset of patients diagnosed with IBS. Thus understanding the role of sleep in pathogenesis and symptom manifestation will provide insights that will allow the clinician to formulate a much more optimal approach to treatment.

The reason for the high prevalence of sleep disorders in CKD patients is not well understood but is likely multifactorial (Fig. 15.5-1). First, the high prevalence of sleep disorders in CKD may be due to a confluence of comorbidities rather than a direct relationship with reduced renal function.
