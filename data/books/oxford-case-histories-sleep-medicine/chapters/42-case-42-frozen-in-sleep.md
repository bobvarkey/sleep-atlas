# Case 42 Frozen in sleep

<!-- pdf-page: 272 -->

A 38-year-old woman presented with a history of being unable to move on waking since her teenage years. This can occur just as she is going to sleep or she can wake in the night with the sensation. She feels unable to move and sometimes, especially in the past, she felt as if she had trouble breathing. On rare occasions, she has felt as if there is someone present, sometimes pressing down on her chest. In the past, she would panic, which seemed to prolong the episode. More recently, the episodes have been briefer and she seems to snap out of them. Often, if she can concentrate on moving a finger or toe, this can end the episode. She can have weeks between episodes, but in recent months, following a change in her job, she is having episodes every night. She thinks her mother may have had similar episodes, but her mother is no longer living and she has a teenage daughter who has started to complain of similar episodes, although occurring infrequently.

#### Answers

#### 1. What is this condition and with what is it associated?

This is isolated sleep paralysis and occurs in about 7% of the population. Usually it develops in the person’s twenties and can be made worse with stress and sleep deprivation. Frequently people panic during the episodes, which represent REM sleep intrusion into wakefulness with associated atonia. There is often a feeling of suffocation, but people do not stop breathing. The events can be associated with a hypnic hallucination (especially in narcolepsy), usually of someone pushing down on the person’s chest. Sleep paralysis can be familial and there is a strong genetic factor. The episodes usually last a matter of seconds or minutes.

#### 2. What investigations would you arrange?

In typical cases without any other symptoms suggestive of narcolepsy, investigation is unnecessary. If investigated, episodes can be associated on PSG with REM features intruding into wakefulness.

Good sleep hygiene, adequate sleep and strategies to reduce stress can all be helpful. During the episodes, concentrating on small movements of a finger or toe can terminate the episode. If medication is required, antidepressants, in particular selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, can be helpful.
