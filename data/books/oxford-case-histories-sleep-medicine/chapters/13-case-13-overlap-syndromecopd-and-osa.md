# Case 13 Overlap syndrome—COPD and OSA

<!-- pdf-page: 102 -->

A 54-year-old man was referred to the sleep clinic by the occupational health department. He was a personal assistant, but had been having difficulty at work with sleepiness and poor attention. He had a prior diagnosis of OSA made five years previously. He had been prescribed CPAP and had good compliance with it. When he was using CPAP, there were no partner reports of snoring. His weight was stable from the time of diagnosis. His only past medical history was of hypertension, for which he took an angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor. He was a smoker of five cigarettes per day and had accumulated a 15 pack year history. He rated himself on the Epworth score as 13/24.

He had good sleep hygiene and adequate sleep duration. There was no indication of narcolepsy or restless legs syndrome.

On examination, he was alert. His SpO2 on room air was 94%. He was obese with a BMI of 47 kg/m2. His chest was clear on auscultation.

Spirometry revealed a significant obstructive defect with FEV1 1.27 litres (37% predicted), FVC 3.23 litres (75% predicted) and an FEV1/ FVC ratio of 39%.

Review of his CPAP machine showed good objective CPAP compliance (average CPAP usage ~ four hours per day).

Arterial blood gases were performed and suggested chronic compensated type 2 respiratory failure (pH 7.34, PO2 7.41 kPa, PCO2 8.22 kPa, HCO3 32.4 mmol/l).

A sleep study was requested while on CPAP and showed that he was hypoventilating during the night (mean SpO2 75%), although there were no significant obstructive events.

<!-- pdf-page: 103 -->

A diagnosis of the overlap syndrome was made. His obstructive lung disease was diagnosed as COPD. He was advised regarding smoking cessation and was prescribed appropriate inhaled therapy.

He was referred for non-invasive ventilation and was titrated to pressures of Inspiratory Positive Airway Pressure (IPAP) with 34 cm of water and Expiratory Positive Airway Pressure (EPAP) with 10 cm of water with 1.5 litres of oxygen entrained overnight. With this therapy, his hypoventilation was treated (mean SpO2 92%) and daytime blood gases significantly improved (pH 7.38, pO2 12.3 kPa, pCO2 6.84 kPa).

When he was seen back at the clinic, he felt much better and was less sleepy. After two months on therapy, he was not experiencing any sleepiness at work and his work performance was deemed to be satisfactory. He was discharged from occupational health follow-up.

#### Answers

#### 1. What is overlap syndrome?

Overlap syndrome is the name given to the combination of COPD and OSA. Studies have suggested a prevalence of the overlap syndrome of 1–2% in adult males. Both conditions are very common and it has been suggested that the presence of either condition increases the chance of finding the other by 10%. There are many postulated mechanisms by which each condition may worsen the other, e.g. steroid use in COPD may contribute to skeletal and diaphragmatic myopathies. In animal models of OSA, it has been noted that repeated upper airway collapse causes increased lower airway resistance, which may worsen the symptoms of COPD.

#### 2. When should overlap syndrome be suspected?

Patients may present with either symptoms of OSA (sleepiness, snoring, witnessed apnoeas) or have a primary diagnosis and symptoms of COPD. However, those with overlap syndrome have been shown to have a much higher prevalence of pulmonary hypertension (16% in OSA alone, 86% in overlap syndrome).

Even patients with severe OSA do not develop severe pulmonary hypertension in the absence of other pathology. Thus, discovery of severe pulmonary hypertension in a patient with OSA should lead one to consider a diagnosis of overlap syndrome. Evidence of pulmonary hypertension out of proportion to the degree of lung disease should prompt similar considerations in patients with COPD.

Patients with overlap syndrome have been found to have a significantly worse quality of life compared with COPD controls as measured by the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire. This may occur even in those without the classical excessive daytime somnolence usually seen in OSA, and suggests that in this group even relatively subtle sleepdisordered breathing may have significant clinical consequences.

Comorbid OSA may also have a mortality effect on COPD. Marin et al. reported that patients with the untreated overlap syndrome had a worse outcome than those with COPD alone, even when corrected for COPD severity.

Thus, it seems reasonable to screen patients with COPD or OSA for evidence of the other conditions, as the mortality associated with overlap syndrome is higher than for either condition alone.

Patients with OSA should be asked about their smoking history. In those with any respiratory symptoms or a significant smoking history, this should prompt formal pulmonary function testing to be performed. In patients with OSA and a significant smoking history who remain sleepy despite good CPAP compliance at an adequate pressure, the diagnosis should be considered.

COPD patients with more pulmonary hypertension than would be expected or those with symptoms suggestive of OSA should be referred for a full assessment by a sleep physician.

Treatment of overlap syndrome aims to treat the underlying disease and any sleep disordered breathing, and to maintain nocturnal oxygenation. COPD should be treated in a standard fashion using bronchodilators, anticholinergics and inhaled steroids as appropriate.

#### 3. How is overlap syndrome treated?

It has been shown that use of nocturnal oxygen alone increases the duration of apnoeic events and increases the end tidal CO2 after each event and thus its use alone is not recommended in overlap syndrome. The mainstay of treatment in overlap syndrome is the use of CPAP with supplemental nocturnal oxygen if required. The study by Marin et al., although not randomized, appears to show mortality benefits for patients compliant with this treatment.

There have been no specific trials on the use of non-invasive ventilation in overlap syndrome, although this therapy has been subject to a number of randomized controlled trials in stable COPD patients with hypercapnia. Results thus far have been conflicting, although it is interesting that one of the positive trials reported is likely to have included patients with overlap syndrome. Further evidence is required regarding the use of and indications for non-invasive ventilation (NIV) in overlap syndrome, although it is often used in patients for whom CPAP alone is inadequate.
